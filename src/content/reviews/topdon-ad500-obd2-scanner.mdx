## How we evaluated this

Nothing in this review comes from holding the device. The basis is the manufacturer's specification sheet and listing copy as it stands today, TopDon's own gateway certification table, the warranty terms published on their US site, and the recurring themes in owner reviews on that listing — paraphrased rather than quoted. Where the listing stays silent on something a buyer would want to know, we name the silence instead of guessing at it.

## What the AD500 actually is

A five-inch Android tablet on the end of an OBD-II cable. Inside: Android 11, 2GB of RAM, 32GB of storage and a 3350mAh internal cell. It reads four systems — engine, transmission, ABS and SRS — and runs all ten OBD2 test modes rather than the emissions subset. TopDon rates it for 67-plus brands and roughly ten thousand models, and it speaks CAN-FD alongside the older protocols.

Set that against the AD600S and the hardware envelope is identical: same screen size, same 1280×720 resolution, same Android version. Whatever separates these two products, it is not the tablet. It is what the software is unlocked to do.

Four systems is the number that matters most, and it is easy to skim past. Every cheap reader handles engine codes, because OBD-II made those a legal requirement. Transmission, ABS and SRS codes are manufacturer-specific and stay invisible to a reader that only speaks the generic protocol. The SRS case is the one that catches people out: an airbag warning light cannot be read or cleared without restraint-system access, and a car that fails inspection on that light is a car you cannot register.

What it cannot do is worth stating as plainly as what it can. There is no bidirectional control, so it will not command an actuator — no cycling the ABS pump during a bleed, no running a fuel pump on demand, no switching a cooling fan to test it. There is no ECU coding and no module programming. The six resets are the full extent of its write access, which makes the phrase "car diagnostic tool" narrower here than the same phrase on a professional unit.

## Six resets, and the three that are not here

The listing names six maintenance functions, and each one exists to switch off a light that appears after a specific job:

- **Oil service** — resets the oil life counter after a change, which is what turns the service light off.
- **Steering angle sensor** — re-centres the reading after an alignment or steering work, without which stability control can fault.
- **TPMS** — registers replacement tyre sensors after a rotation or a new set of wheels.
- **Battery registration** — tells the charging system that a new battery is fitted, so it stops charging to the old battery's profile.
- **Electronic parking brake** — retracts the calipers so pads can be changed at all.
- **Throttle adaptation** — relearns idle after the throttle body is cleaned or replaced.

The [AD600S](/reviews/topdon-ad600s-obd2-scanner/) carries nine. The three it adds are diesel particulate filter regeneration, ABS bleeding and injector coding.

Whether those three matter is not a matter of taste. DPF regeneration is a diesel problem; a petrol owner will never open that menu. ABS bleeding only enters the picture after work on the hydraulic circuit. Injector coding comes after replacing an injector. If none of those describe your year, the extra $36 buys functions that sit unused — which is the case for most people shopping at this price.

## Gateway access, on the older branch

TopDon publishes a table of models certified for FCA's security gateway, and this scanner appears on it as ArtiDiag500, at app version V1.31 with brand software at V1.90 for Chrysler, Dodge and Jeep and V2.30 for Fiat. The AD600S appears on the same table at app V1.1.7 with V10.48 across those three brands and V10.64 for Fiat.

Both are certified. They are not on the same software generation, and the version numbers are the evidence: the 600S sits on the V10.4x line while this one sits on V1.9x. Coverage updates for gateway work therefore arrive on the slower of the two tracks. For someone who rarely touches a 2017-or-newer Stellantis vehicle this changes nothing; for someone who does, it is a reason to look at the higher model.

The fee attached to that access is worth stating plainly, because no listing mentions it: AutoAuth charges $50 a year per shop, covering six users, and bills the card directly. TopDon is not part of that transaction.

## What it costs to own

The purchase price is the whole of TopDon's ask. No software renewal, no cartridge, no account beyond the first setup, and updates arrive over Wi-Fi for as long as the hardware lasts.

Two optional plans sit at checkout, and both are single payments rather than subscriptions — the listing data marks each as a one-time charge. Three years of cover adds $25.99; four years adds $34.99. That puts three years of covered use at $185.98, or about $62 a year. Spread the four-year plan over five years and the annual figure falls to roughly $39, with the fifth year uncovered.

The third offer is the one to decline. Complete Protect bills $16.99 every month, which is $203.88 a year on a device that costs $159.99 — more than the scanner itself, annually, for cover that applies across an account rather than to this tool.

Set that against the [AD600S](/reviews/topdon-ad600s-obd2-scanner/), whose three-year plan costs $28.99, so the same period there runs to $224.98. The gap widens from $36 at the till to $39 across three years — small enough that price alone should not decide between them.

The one recurring charge no listing mentions applies to a specific group. Vehicles from 2017 onward under Chrysler, Ram, Dodge, Jeep or Fiat sit behind a security gateway that needs an AutoAuth registration at $50 a year, billed by AutoAuth rather than TopDon. It applies whichever of these two scanners you buy.

## Where the marketing outruns the datasheet

**"Brand-new" is a page label, not a platform generation.** The listing calls this the brand-new ArtiDiag500 and says it is back in full swing. TopDon's own gateway certification table places the plain ArtiDiag500 at application version V1.31, with brand software at V1.90 for Chrysler, Dodge and Jeep. The AD600S sits on the same table at V1.1.7 with V10.48. Both are certified, they are not on the same software line, and the model carrying the S suffix runs the newer one.

**Gateway access is advertised without its price.** AutoAuth appears in the feature list as though it were included. The $50 annual registration is the difference between a feature that works on the first evening and one that needs paperwork first.

**"Free for life" against two accounts of being asked to pay.** Updates are described as free for the life of the device, and the manufacturer's standard warranty runs twelve months. Two owners separately describe being asked to pay before certain functions would work on newer vehicles. We could not confirm a subscription exists for this model, so those stay as unresolved reports rather than a cost to budget for.

**Battery powered, network required.** The specification table lists the power source as the battery. Updating needs Wi-Fi, and the listing publishes no runtime figure, so how long a charge lasts is something you learn in use rather than from the page.

## What owner feedback adds

Three themes recur. Setup and screen legibility draw consistent praise, including from owners using it on multiple vehicles. Connection problems appear often enough to be a pattern rather than an outlier — some units fail to link on first attempt, and in the cases described, support resolved it remotely. Coverage complaints cluster around specific models where a particular system did not respond, which is the normal shape of these complaints at any price.

The recurring complaint worth separating from the rest is the network dependency. Nobody describes being charged for an update; some describe the device wanting a connection when they wanted a code read. Those are different problems, and only the second one is a design constraint.

## How it compares

Against the [AD600S](/reviews/topdon-ad600s-obd2-scanner/) at $195.99: same four systems, same screen, same Android version, three more resets. The $36 buys diesel particulate filter regeneration, ABS bleeding and injector coding — work that otherwise means a shop visit or a tool you do not own.

Against the [TOPDON ONE](/reviews/topdon-one-bidirectional-scan-tool/) at $839: the ONE commands actuators, codes modules and maps topology, and carries a $299 annual software fee. Nothing here competes with that. The two are different classes of purchase separated by a factor of five, and the comparison is only useful for deciding whether you need the class above.

Against a basic code reader: those clear engine codes and show live data for a fraction of the money. What the extra spend buys is three more systems and six resets, and nothing else.

## Who should buy it

Buy the AD500 if you want four-system reads, the six routine resets, and no renewal to think about — and if your vehicles are petrol and your brake work stops at pads. Buy the [AD600S](/reviews/topdon-ad600s-obd2-scanner/) instead if a diesel is in the driveway, if you bleed brakes yourself, or if injector work is on the horizon; those three functions are the entire difference, and three years of cover on either runs within $39 of the other.
