## How we evaluated this

Nothing in this review comes from a styling session we ran ourselves. We worked from wavytalk's product listing and instruction text, the published specification set for model B390, current retail pricing across US retailers, and the themes that recur across 5,527 owner ratings. Manufacturer figures are labelled as such. Where our arithmetic and the listing disagree, we show the working rather than picking a side.

## What the Steamline Pro is

Model B390 is a corded heated brush: a toothed comb head with a steam feed behind it, four heat steps, and a body that stays cool enough to bring close to the scalp. The pitch is that you brush sections straight in one pass while steam keeps the cuticle from drying out, instead of clamping hair between two hot plates.

The box is more complete than the listing's structured data suggests — that field lists only "hair clip" under included components, but what ships is the brush, a heat-resistant glove, two sectioning clips, a small cleaning brush, a dropper bottle for water, and a scrunchie. Nothing extra to buy on day one.

## The steam claim, checked against the tank

wavytalk rates steam output at 0.5 ml per minute. Run that for thirty minutes and you have used about 15 ml of water, so a reservoir that genuinely covers the claimed session has to hold roughly that much.

Owner feedback points to real sessions running longer than the claim assumes. People with very thick or type 4 hair describe refilling two, three or four times to finish a head, and one times the session at 45 minutes. That is not a fault so much as arithmetic: more hair means more passes, more passes means more minutes, and the tank is sized for the shorter session.

Distilled water is not a suggestion. The manual asks for it specifically to stop mineral build-up blocking the nozzles — and a blocked nozzle is the failure that quietly ends most steam brushes.

## The four percentages

The listing leads with 101% shinier, 77% smoother, 62% less frizz and 27% more resistance to breakage, all against "traditional flat irons". None of the four comes with a test protocol, a sample size, an instrument or a time frame. Shine is usually measured on a swatch with a glossmeter, and a figure above 100% means the measured value doubled — a strong claim to publish without the method.

That does not make the claims false. Steam-assisted styling does reduce the scorched-hair smell and the dryness people associate with daily plate ironing, and owners describe exactly that. It means the numbers are marketing rather than measurement, and the thing worth weighing is the mechanism — moisture before heat — not the percentages.

## The temperature ladder contradicts the spec line

The listing's feature text says four settings spanning 325°F to 410°F. The device's own printed ladder, legible in wavytalk's product photography, reads 410 / 380 / 350 / 320 — so the bottom step is 320°F, not 325°F. A small discrepancy, but it changes the answer for one group of users.

The safety section of the same listing advises keeping the tool at no more than 320°F for fine, thin or fragile hair. That is exactly the lowest setting, which leaves fine-haired users one usable step and no headroom underneath it: if 320°F still feels hot on your hair, there is nothing lower to select.

Read that as a design statement rather than a defect. The tool is built for medium to coarse textures, and fine hair is being accommodated in the manual rather than in the hardware. If your hair is fine or fragile, the honest conclusion is that this is the wrong tool, not a tool to use carefully.

## What it is genuinely good at

**Roots and edges.** The teeth are guarded and the housing runs cooler than a plate, so you can work close to the scalp without flinching. Owners mention this consistently, and it is the one thing the design does that a flat iron structurally cannot.

**Speed on coarse hair.** Multiple owners report styling time dropping from around 25 minutes to under 10, or from four hours to under two. Those are owner reports, not our measurements, but the direction is consistent across textures and hair types.

**The cost side.** No subscription, no app, no filter cartridge, no proprietary refill. Distilled water is the only consumable, and it costs very little. For a styling tool that is an unusually clean ownership picture.

## Where it falls short

**Ends.** A brush cannot clamp and pull the way a plate can, so the last few centimetres usually need an extra pass or a second tool. Owners raise this more than any other limitation.

**Silk press.** Straight is not the same as pressed. People with type 4 hair describe a smooth, manageable result rather than the mirror finish of a salon press.

**Shape.** It straightens. It does not curl, wave or flick. If you want one tool for several styles, this is the wrong geometry.

**Weight and cord.** Around 1.15 lb with a cord attached and no battery option, so it is a bathroom-counter tool rather than a travel-afterthought — dual voltage helps abroad, but you still need a plug adapter for the US polarized plug.

## How it compares

Against a flat iron: slower per section, safer near the scalp, gentler on damp hair, weaker on ends. Against a hot air brush: less volume, more straightening. Against a salon blowout every fortnight: the break-even arrives quickly, which is the argument most owners actually make.

## Who should buy it

Buy it if you have thick, curly, coily or 4C hair, you style it yourself, and the bottleneck is the twenty to forty minutes of sectioning rather than the final result. Buy it if you want to stop booking a blowout every two weeks.

Skip it if your hair is fine or fragile — the temperature floor sits above your ceiling. Skip it if you want silk-press results or a tool that also curls.
