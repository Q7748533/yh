## How we evaluated this

We have not run this vacuum. What follows comes from the retail listing as it reads today, the manufacturer's own product page and FAQ, the arithmetic those two sources make possible, and the themes that repeat across the owner reviews on the listing — summarised rather than quoted. Where the sources disagree with each other, we show both numbers instead of picking the flattering one.

## What the Nova V5 is

A cordless stick vacuum with a 1.8-litre bin, two suction modes, a motorised floor head and a touch screen that reports battery level. The body stands upright without a bracket, which matters more than it sounds: most stick vacuums at this price have to be leaned against a wall or hung, and a vacuum you cannot park is a vacuum you stop using.

The bin is the standout figure. At $120 the field usually gives you half a litre, which means emptying twice in a single pass over a carpeted room. 1.8 litres is closer to what a corded upright carries.

Filtration is cyclonic with a washable HEPA element, and the listing claims 99.99% capture. No micron rating and no HEPA class appear anywhere on the listing or the manufacturer's site, so the percentage cannot be checked against a standard — it is a number without a test behind it.

## The three headline numbers cannot all be true

The title of the listing carries three figures: 60KPa, 650W and 70 minutes. Take them one at a time and each is plausible. Put them together with the battery the machine ships with and they stop being compatible.

The pack is eight 2500mAh cells. The replacement chargers sold for this model specify 29.6V, which puts the pack at about 74 watt-hours.

Run the arithmetic in both directions and the same answer appears. At a genuine 650W, 74 watt-hours lasts under seven minutes — not twenty, and nowhere near seventy. Work backwards from the runtimes instead and the machine is drawing about 222W at maximum and about 63W at eco. Those are ordinary numbers for a cordless stick vacuum, and nothing about them is a defect.

So what is 650W? Most likely a peak or motor-input rating, the kind of figure that is technically true for a fraction of a second and meaningless as a description of cleaning power. The problem is not that the number is invented. It is that the listing places it next to a runtime that contradicts it, and a buyer comparing 650W against a rival's 450W is comparing something the battery will never deliver.

## What the brand's own site says about suction

The manufacturer's page lists the Nova V5 twice. One entry reads 48KPa with 65 minutes. The other, described as the upgraded version, reads 55KPa with a 650W motor and up to 70 minutes.

The Amazon listing says 60KPa. That is not either of them.

We cannot tell you which figure is current. It is entirely possible that a third revision exists and the site has not caught up, or that the listing rounds up from the upgraded variant. What we can say is that the highest number on the listing is the only place that number appears, and that the brand's own material tops out at 55KPa. If you are comparing this against another vacuum's kPa figure, use the lower published number and you will not be disappointed by the difference.

## Self-standing, and the wall mount you are told you do not need

The second bullet sells the stand-up body as storage and charging without wall mounting. The same listing's feature summary, a few lines further down, lists wall-mounted charging.

The manufacturer's FAQ is more cautious than either: it says many stick vacuums do not free-stand securely, and advises using the included wall mount or laying the unit down to prevent tipping. That is the brand hedging against its own product page.

Owners land where you would expect. On hard floors it stands. On carpet, one reviewer describes it as a little wobbly and says it fell over, which they rated as not a big deal. Treat self-standing as a hard-floor convenience, not a guarantee.

## Two smaller disagreements

**Noise.** The first bullet says the vacuum runs under 58dB. The specification block lists low noise at 62dB or below. Both are in the same listing, four decibels apart, and four decibels is roughly the difference between a quiet conversation and a vacuum cleaner.

**The screen.** The title advertises an OLED touch screen. The bullet describing it calls it an LED intelligent touch screen. OLED and LED are different panels with different contrast behaviour, and only one of those words is right.

Neither affects how well it cleans. Both tell you something about how carefully the listing was assembled, which is worth knowing before you trust the numbers that do affect cleaning.

## What it costs to own

The vacuum is $119.96 today, marked down 25% from a $159.99 list. A three-year protection plan adds $21.99, which puts three years of covered use at $141.95.

The battery is the number to plan around. A replacement pack sells for $50, which is 42% of what the machine costs today, and every cordless vacuum reaches the point where a two-year-old cell holds half a charge. Buying a second battery early is the difference between a vacuum that covers a whole house and one that covers a room.

Filters are the good news. The element is washable, and compatible replacements run $18 to $21 for a multi-pack, so filtration is not a subscription the way it is on some rivals.

## What owner feedback adds

The rating sits at 4.6 across a large sample, and the praise is unusually consistent for this category. Pet hair comes up. The light on the floor head reveals hair along skirting boards and under furniture. Assembly takes minutes. Emptying the bin is a one-touch job that does not require reaching into the debris.

The complaints are specific rather than systemic. One owner found it wobbly on carpet. One wanted a wider tool for stairs and ended up using the whole machine on them. One had a battery fail early and had it replaced by the seller without argument, which is worth weighing when the replacement costs $50.

Nobody in the reviews we read describes the suction as weak, which is the failure that usually defines this price band.

## Who should buy it

Buy it if your floors are mostly hard, your carpets are low-pile, and the job is daily hair and crumbs rather than a weekly deep clean. The bin size and the stand-up body are genuine advantages at $120, and the anti-tangle brush addresses the failure that kills cheap stick vacuums.

Do not buy it expecting the 650W or the 60KPa to mean what they look like they mean. If you need sustained suction for embedded carpet dirt, a corded upright at the same money will outperform any battery machine in this class, and it will still be working in ten years.
