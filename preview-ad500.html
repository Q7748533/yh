TL;DR

The AD500 is a $160 four-system reader with six maintenance resets, lifetime updates and a 12-month warranty. The catch is narrow and specific: the $196 AD600S adds three resets — DPF regeneration, ABS bleeding and injector coding — for $36 more. Two listings sell this same scanner $13 apart, so check both before buying. Its gateway software also sits on an older branch than the 600S, which matters if you work on 2017-and-newer FCA vehicles.

How we evaluated this

Nothing in this review comes from holding the device. The basis is the manufacturer’s specification sheet and listing copy as it stands today, TopDon’s own gateway certification table, the warranty terms published on their US site, and the recurring themes in owner reviews on that listing — paraphrased rather than quoted. Where the listing stays silent on something a buyer would want to know, we name the silence instead of guessing at it.

What the AD500 actually is

A five-inch Android tablet on the end of an OBD-II cable. Inside: Android 11, 2GB of RAM, 32GB of storage and a 3350mAh internal cell. It reads four systems — engine, transmission, ABS and SRS — and runs all ten OBD2 test modes rather than the emissions subset. TopDon rates it for 67-plus brands and roughly ten thousand models, and it speaks CAN-FD alongside the older protocols.

Set that against the AD600S and the hardware envelope is identical: same screen size, same 1280×720 resolution, same Android version. Whatever separates these two products, it is not the tablet. It is what the software is unlocked to do.

Six resets, and the three that are not here

The listing names six maintenance functions: oil service, steering angle sensor, TPMS, battery registration, electronic parking brake and throttle adaptation.

The AD600S carries nine. The three it adds are diesel particulate filter regeneration, ABS bleeding and injector coding.

Whether those three matter is not a matter of taste. DPF regeneration is a diesel problem; a petrol owner will never open that menu. ABS bleeding only enters the picture after work on the hydraulic circuit. Injector coding comes after replacing an injector. If none of those describe your year, the extra $36 buys functions that sit unused — which is the case for most people shopping at this price.

The same scanner, two listings, $13 apart

At the time of writing, one catalogue entry carried $159.99 with no promotion attached, and a second entry for the same ArtiDiag500 carried $146.99 marked down 17% from an identical $178 list price. Neither page offers the other as an alternative.

The pattern repeats across TopDon’s range: the AD600S also sells under two entries, $10 apart. It is worth thirty seconds of searching before checkout, because the saving is larger than most coupon codes.

Gateway access, on the older branch

TopDon publishes a table of models certified for FCA’s security gateway, and this scanner appears on it as ArtiDiag500, at app version V1.31 with brand software at V1.90 for Chrysler, Dodge and Jeep and V2.30 for Fiat. The AD600S appears on the same table at app V1.1.7 with V10.48 across those three brands and V10.64 for Fiat.

Both are certified. They are not on the same software generation, and the version numbers are the evidence: the 600S sits on the V10.4x line while this one sits on V1.9x. Coverage updates for gateway work therefore arrive on the slower of the two tracks. For someone who rarely touches a 2017-or-newer Stellantis vehicle this changes nothing; for someone who does, it is a reason to look at the higher model.

The fee attached to that access is worth stating plainly, because no listing mentions it: AutoAuth charges $50 a year per shop, covering six users, and bills the card directly. TopDon is not part of that transaction.

What owner feedback adds

Three themes recur. Setup and screen legibility draw consistent praise, including from owners using it on multiple vehicles. Connection problems appear often enough to be a pattern rather than an outlier — some units fail to link on first attempt, and in the cases described, support resolved it remotely. Coverage complaints cluster around specific models where a particular system did not respond, which is the normal shape of these complaints at any price.

Two owners separately describe being asked to pay before certain functions would work on newer vehicles. That is the one claim we could not resolve either way: the listing calls the updates free for life, and we found no published subscription for this model. We are reporting it as an unresolved owner account, not as a hidden cost.

Who should buy it

Buy the AD500 if you want four-system reads, the six routine resets, and no renewal to think about — and if your vehicles are petrol and your brake work stops at pads. Buy the AD600S instead if a diesel is in the driveway, if you bleed brakes yourself, or if injector work is on the horizon; those three functions are the entire difference.

Either way, search the model name before you check out. The cheaper listing is real, and it is not advertised on the expensive one.

Verdict

The AD500 is the right buy for a home mechanic who wants four-system reads and the six resets that routine maintenance actually calls for. It does those jobs at $160 with no renewal, which is the whole argument. Buy the $196 AD600S instead only if you own a diesel, plan to bleed brakes yourself, or will be coding injectors — those are the three functions the extra $36 unlocks, and nothing else in the comparison changes. Whichever you pick, open both listings first: the same scanner was on sale $13 cheaper under a second ASIN.

Frequently asked questions

Does the AD500 need a subscription?

No. The listing describes the updates as free for life over Wi-Fi, and the standard TopDon warranty runs twelve months from purchase. That said, two owners on the listing describe being asked to pay before certain functions would work on newer vehicles. We looked for a published subscription for this model and did not find one, so treat those reports as unresolved rather than as a hidden fee you should budget for. What is documented and separate is AutoAuth: if you work on 2017-or-newer Chrysler, Ram, Dodge, Jeep or Fiat vehicles, the security gateway needs a registration that costs $50 a year, paid to AutoAuth rather than to TopDon.

What does the AD600S have that the AD500 does not?

Three maintenance resets: diesel particulate filter regeneration, ABS bleeding and injector coding. The AD600S carries nine permanent service functions against six here. Everything else in the hardware envelope is the same — both use a five-inch 1280×720 screen, both run Android 11, and both read engine, transmission, ABS and SRS. The $36 difference buys those three functions and nothing more.

Can the AD500 clear codes on any car?

It reads and clears engine codes on any OBD-II compliant vehicle from 1996 onward in the United States, which is a legal requirement rather than a feature. The four-system diagnostics and the six resets depend on manufacturer coverage, and TopDon rates the tool for 67-plus brands. Coverage is not universal: owners report specific models where a system did not respond, so check your vehicle against the current coverage list before relying on a particular reset.

Why are there two listings for the same scanner?

TopDon maintains separate catalogue entries for what is the same device, and they are priced independently. At the time of writing one entry carried $159.99 with no promotion while another carried $146.99 marked down from the same $178 list price. Neither page points at the other, so the only way to find the cheaper entry is to search the model name and compare.

Is the AD500 worth it over a cheaper code reader?

It depends on whether you need the resets. A basic reader clears engine codes and shows live data for a fraction of the price. What the AD500 adds is access to four systems rather than one, and six service functions that switch off warning lights and re-teach components after maintenance. If your work stops at reading a check-engine light, the extra money is not doing anything for you.
